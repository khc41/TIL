## 📅 2026-07-14
### 📖 Tech blog: [Building Service Topology at Scale: Architecture Challenges and Lessons Learned](https://netflixtechblog.com/building-service-topology-at-scale-architecture-challenges-and-lessons-learned-f4b792f3f0d8)
#### ✏️ Summation
- They faced challenges in their production environment. service topology work perfectly only in local environment.
- at their scale, they faced issues like kafka consumers falling behind instances running out of memory, some nodes receiving 100x the traffic of others, and garbage collection pauses consuming more CPU than actual business logic.
- They felt limitations in traditional methods like unbounded queues, drop-based flow control, and batch processing.
- They resolved the hot node issues by decoupling responsibilities, allowing State 2 to focus exclusively on resolution and load redistribution.   
- They initially used gRPC. But it became a performance bottleneck, serialization overhead, connection pool management, and memory pressure for streaming responses consumed more CPU than business logic.
- They leveraged their existing service registry to handle hashing, successfully resolving the issue without introducing additional infrastructure.
- Kafka lags kept increasing because flow logs were accumulating faster than the consumers could process them. they resolved this by increasing the number of Kafka partitions and reducing the number of network round-trips.
- They realized that optimizing in isolation at scale is impossible, as resolving one bottleneck only exposes another downstream.
- The key insight: architectural decisions driven by one requirement (proxy resolution) often solve other problems (load distribution) as beneficial side effects.
- “Best practices” are starting points, not absolute rules.
- The lesson: be willing to break conventional wisdom when scale justifies it. But do it based on measurement.
- Each optimization raises throughput, which stresses the next weakest point.

#### 💡 Reflections
- It reminded me of the stream processing concepts I read about in DDIA, not batch processing for real-time processing.
- I thought that it would be better to process stream data with a delay of a few seconds (not very real-time) if the loss rate of drop-based is tolerable, but if consistency is critical, it might be better to display a timestamp like 'updated a few minutes ago' just like how YouTube indicates whether a video is truly live or 20 seconds behind.
- I wondered how pipeline detects automatically slowing down due to backpressure and GC Pause.
- When I saw consistent hashing, it reminded me of the ring hashing I read about for system design interview.
- I wondered how they efficiently aggregate data within 5-minutes time windows. Given the massive scale of millions of requests per second, I truly realized how crucial efficient algorithms become as data volume grows.
- They handled the data by breaking it into three stages to eliminate intermediaries-windowing, mapping by from-to pairs, and then the final aggregation. It really felt like solving an algorithm problem where you divide a big problem into smaller pieces.
- They explained that the main reason for splitting the process into three stages is data locality, drawing a parallel to how MapReduce sorts massive datasets to solve locality issues. Realizing this made me think: once you truly grasp the fundamental principles of a system, you can apply them to solve problems almost anywhere.
- When I saw 'This highlights a key principle: data partitioning strategy determines processing architecture', it immediately made me think of what I read in DDIA.
- Seeing how they tuned fetch parameters-trading off per-message latency for higher throughput by fetching larger batches-immediately reminded me of my recent contribution to Spring Cloud AWS's SqsAsyncBatchManager. It was a great reminder that the core concept of batching to reduce network round-trips applies universally across message queues and streaming platforms.
- Their transition from gRPC to SSE proved once again that there is no silver bullet in system architecture. Decisions must be guided by actual measurements and concrete results, not by industry hype or team familiarity. Looking ahead, as AI drastically lowers the entry barrier, the development and evolution of highly scalable distributed systems will only accelerate.
- In standard OOP or functional programming, we are taught to favor immutability to prevent unexpected state changes. However, seeing how they compromised and opted for mutable structures to optimize memory resources at scale was fascinating. It proved that at millions of records per second, the overhead of creating new immutable objects with every update can completely overwhelm the garbage collector, making mutability a necessary engineering trade-off.
- Their decision to standardize on JSON encoding over binary serialization was highly impressive. It's easy to obsess over micro-optimizations at scale, but choosing human-readable JSON to drastically simplify debugging and eliminate a whole class of serialization bugs shows true pragmatic engineering. It proved that sometimes, developer velocity and system observability are worth the negligible performance trade-offs.
- The 'Measure, Hypothesize, Validate, Iterate' cycle in V2 deeply resonated with my daily routine of monitoring Grafana, formulating hypotheses, and continuously tuning performance. This realization made me crave even bigger challenges; I realized that to encounter and solve truly complex, system-level bottlenecks, I need to immerse myself in an environment with even massive scales of traffic.
